Lab 1: Introduction to AI with Coin Game  

For 4.1.3, consider including the arrows on the completed table here in the TG like Dan did in the PD (see my DM to JK on Slack 7/30). --MF, 7/30/26

To introduce AI, students first spend time playing a hands-on coin game:

  • Start with 10 coins.
  • Take turns. On your turn, take either 1 or 2 coins.
  • Whoever takes the last coin wins!
Students seek the best strategy by researching which positions allow the player to win and then analyze code for an AI coin game strategy seeker and complete key unfinished blocks. This type of algorithm is often described as "good old fashion AI" (a kind of AI from before the AI boom of the 2020s). In the most advanced labs of this unit, students explore AI coin game strategy seeking algorithms that use a different kind of AI: reinforcement learning (e.g., Q-learning).

Pacing

This lab may take your students about 2–7 class periods (60–300 minutes).
  • Activity 1: Playing the Coin Game 20–90 minutes
  • Activity 2: Coin Game Strategies 10–60 minutes
  • Activity 3: Discovering the Win/Lose Pattern 10–60 minutes
  • Activity 4: Coding the Coin Game 20–90 minutes

Activity 1: Playing the Coin Game 


  • Materials: Playing the Coin Game student page
  • Prepare:
    • Each pair of students will need 10 counters (e.g., coins).
    • Consider working through Activity 4 before or early in your teaching of this lab so you understand the AI algorithm students are working towards throughout the lab.
  • Learning Goals:
    • Students build intuition for the coin game.
  • Activity Plan:
    • Hand out counters and explain the rules. (less than 5 minutes)
    • Students play repeatedly, taking turns who starts first and playing against other players. (ideally, 1–2 classes)
  • Tips:
    • The next page is all about building a strategy, so no need to introduce strategy seeking as they are just learning the game, though students may naturally think about strategy.
  • Standards:
    • None covered in this activity since is an off line activity that sets the stage for using a computer to seek a game-playing strategy.

Activity 2: Coin Game Strategies 

  • Materials: Coin Game Strategies student page
  • Prepare:
    • Students may benefit from access to their preferred writing method (paper, notebook, word processor) for notes and strategy documentation.
  • Learning Goals:
    • Students develop, test, and refine a strategy for the coin game.
  • Activity Plan:
    • Students reflect on and describe their strategies, compare and compete their strategies, and consider which strategies work best.
  • Tips:
    • There are different ways to think about the coin game which can result in different-looking theories that are still fundamentally implementing the optimal strategy (spoiler hint).
      One way to think of the optimal strategy: always give the other player a multiple of 3 coins so that you can give them another multiple of 3 next time by playing the opposite of their play until you take the last coin(s) giving them the lowest possible multiple (0).
  • Standards:
    • None covered in this activity since is an off line activity that sets the stage for using a computer to seek a game-playing strategy.

  • Materials: Discovering the Win/Lose Pattern student page
  • Prepare:
    • Get clear on how the activity uses the labels "Win" and "Lose" before teaching. While simple, confusion about these definitions can distract key learning. A winning position is what you want to receive on your turn; you want to leave your opponent with a losing position.
  • Learning Goals:
    • Students precisely document a strategy for ideal game-play.
  • Activity Plan:
    • Students consider which positions allow a perfect player to win and which guarantee they will lose against another perfect player.
  • Tips:
    • Consider wrapping up this activity page with a class discussion where the students reason through the table themselves on the blackboard (or whiteboard) and find the strategy together.
    • Another way to think about coin game strategy is to consider the ideal move in each position (1 or 2). While valid, this activity presents the win/lose approach to prepare students for the coin game strategy Snap! project they will soon analyze.
  • Standards:
    • None covered in this activity since is an off line activity that sets the stage for using a computer to seek a game-playing strategy.

Activity 4: Coding the Coin Game 

  • Materials: Coding the Coin Game student page
  • Prepare:
    • Before teaching, work through the student page, reviewing the code and completing the unfinished blocks to specification. Aim to understand how the initialize and get machine move blocks use each of the blocks that the students complete (above) to create the win/lose pattern table.
      Maybe include a walk-through of these blocks on the TG. See the recording of Dan's talk right before the first break on 7/30. --MF, 7/30/26

  • Learning Goals:
    • Students learn how an algorithm can figure out a game-play strategy.
  • Activity Plan:
    • Students analyze nearly complete code for a "good-old-fashioned AI" algorithm for finding the win/lose pattern in the coin game.
    • Students complete the code for the AI algorithm by following specifications for unfinished blocks that highlight the key processes in the AI algorithm.
  • Tips:
    • Students may not achieve perfect understanding of the AI algorithm. The key idea is that code they could understand can find a game-play strategy just like they did in previous activities.
  • Standards:
    • 2-AP-13: Decompose problems and subproblems into parts to facilitate the design, implementation, and review of programs.

  • Solutions
    Include the specs for each block from the student materials. --MF, 7/30/26

Correlation with CSTA Standards 

  • 2-AP-13: Decompose problems and subproblems into parts to facilitate the design, implementation, and review of programs. Students should break down problems into subproblems, which can be further broken down to smaller parts. Decomposition facilitates aspects of program development by allowing students to focus on one piece at a time (e.g., getting input from the user, processing the data, and displaying the result to the user). Decomposition also enables different students to work on different parts at the same time. For example, animations can be decomposed into multiple scenes, which can be developed independently.
